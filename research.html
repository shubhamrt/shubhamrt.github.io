Research

Connectivity, Degradation
and Landscape Resilience

Understanding landscapes as
connected systems

Drylands are fragile. Remove too much vegetation, disturb the soil in the wrong place, and the damage doesn't stay local, it spreads. My research is about understanding how that spread happens and whether we can see it coming before it becomes impossible to reverse.

I use network science to study landscapes: mapping the connections between patches of soil and vegetation the way you might map roads between cities. When enough of those connections link up, a small local disturbance can cascade across an entire hillslope. Finding where that tipping point lies is the central question driving my work.

Beyond drylands, I apply the same thinking to other environmental systems, from how rainfall patterns shift over time to how rivers stay connected for wildlife. I also work in operational hydrology at the UK Environment Agency, where science meets real decisions about floods and droughts.

Current and recent research themes:

  • Dryland connectivity and land degradation How bare soil patches link up to drive erosion and what network structure tells us about a landscape's stability
  • Dust and wind erosion Mapping wind erosion hotspots across the western USA using satellite data and field sensors
  • Vegetation, water flow and erosion How plant patterns shape runoff and sediment movement, measured with drones and aerial surveys
  • Rainfall pattern analysis Using network tools to reconstruct missing rainfall data and understand how patterns shift over decades
  • Networks across environmental systems Theoretical frameworks for understanding how multiple environmental processes interact and co-evolve
  • Operational hydrology Flood forecasting, drought monitoring and river management science applied at the scale of real decisions

Arizona, USA · Dec 2022

Sonoran Desert Rangeland

A semi-arid rangeland where grasses give way to shrubs as the land degrades. This site provided key field evidence for understanding how resource flow and plant patterns interact across a hillslope.

image3.png
Key Concept

Islands of Fertility

In degraded drylands, shrubs become magnets for water and nutrients, creating fertile patches surrounded by bare, exhausted soil. This patterning is one of the clearest signs a landscape is in trouble and a key signal in my research.

image4.png
Key Concept

Length of Connected Pathways

A measure of how far bare soil patches link up across a hillslope. Short, broken pathways mean disturbance stays local. Long, connected ones mean erosion can run uninterrupted, a warning sign of approaching collapse.

image6.png
Key Concept

The Tipping Point

When bare patches connect beyond a critical threshold, sediment transport stops being local and becomes landscape-wide. Erosion accelerates sharply. Identifying where that threshold sits before it is crossed, is at the heart of early-warning detection.
